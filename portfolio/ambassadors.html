Organizational information flow study: CU student ambassadors

How does information move through an organization, and where does it break down? We investigated the tools, workflows, and communication practices used by CU Boulder's Student Ambassador program to identify inefficiencies and opportunities for improvement.

Role

UX Researcher | Team Project

Tools

Miro

Methods

Semi-Structured Interviews, Qualitative Analysis, Thematic Coding, Data Journey Mapping, Social World Mapping

Timeline

Fall 2024

The question

CU Student Ambassadors rely on multiple digital tools and communication channels to coordinate campus tours and student visits. We wanted to understand how information flows between ambassadors, program coordinators, and prospective students, and identify where communication gaps and inefficient processes create challenges.

What I did

  1. Stakeholder Interviews: Collaborated on semi-structured interviews with four stakeholders, including student ambassadors, program leadership, and coordinators, to understand their responsibilities, workflows, and information needs.
  2. Information Systems Analysis: Examined tools and information sources such as WhenToWork, Microsoft Teams, Slate, registration forms, and scheduling documents to understand how information is collected, shared, and managed.
  3. Workflow Mapping: Created and consolidated data journey maps and social world models in Miro to visualize information flow, stakeholder relationships, and dependencies across the organization.
  4. Qualitative Synthesis: Compared interview findings and secondary research to identify recurring challenges, communication breakdowns, and inefficiencies.
  5. Research Recommendations: Translated findings into opportunities for improving scheduling coordination, visitor communication, and manual data processing.

Key findings

  • Manual processes slowed information management. Program coordinators manually cross-checked visitor names and email information for 600+ guests in Excel, creating repetitive administrative work.
  • Scheduling required constant coordination. Frequently changing student schedules and shift coverage requests created challenges, with ambassadors relying on Microsoft Teams to find replacements.
  • Repeated communication did not always prevent confusion. Despite receiving multiple parking reminders, approximately 10% of visitors still experienced confusion about parking locations and instructions.

Recommendations

  • Streamline data processing: Explore automating repetitive visitor data validation to reduce manual work and improve efficiency.
  • Improve shift coordination: Create a more centralized process for communicating schedule changes, requesting shift coverage, and tracking availability.
  • Make visitor instructions more accessible: Simplify parking communications using clearer directions, visual maps, and prominently displayed information before visits.
